# R5 — on real attacks with a clean benign baseline, how often and how fast are they detected, at a fixed false-alarm budget?

## 0. What is locked, what is sealed, what R5 may and may not use
- **Frozen, unchanged:** `research-v1`, R1, R2 (`sparse-r2`) and R3/R4 (`observation-r3r4`) with their manifests. R5 code lives in `ml/` only; nothing under `backend/`, `models/` or `contracts/` changes.
- **Sealed R2 test data stays sealed and is never used again, for anything:** UNSW days 16-10-13 and 16-10-14, and the first 100 MB of IoT-23 Malware-7-1, -17-1, -33-1, -35-1, -36-1, -39-1, -44-1, -52-1, -60-1.
- **R2 development/validation data** (IoT-23 Honeypot-4-1, -5-1, Malware-42-1, -20-1, -21-1, -8-1, -34-1, -3-1, -1-1, -9-1, -49-1, Somfy-01; UNSW 16-10-12) may be used again only as development data or as part of R5's validation benign, never as test.
- **IoT-23 as a whole is expected to be ineligible** (R2: every never-opened capture was malicious from its first minutes; the long-baseline captures 20-1 and 21-1 have only 6 and 10 malicious ticks). It is not planned on. It may serve as a *source of attack profiles* for the synthetic overlay (section 6), development captures only.

## 1. Question
R2 left two things open: whether the false-alarm budget holds on enough held-out exposure (28 device-days gave 2.36 [0.67, 4.54]), and **how well attacks are detected**: no test episodes existed, and on validation every arm caught about 5 of 19 episodes at the 3.0/day budget. R5 measures both on data built to answer them.

## 2. Arms (fixed now, nothing tuned)
**R1-recal**, **A** (zero-inflated) and **B** (windowed-rate): the detectors, constants and code of R2 (`sparse-r2`), unchanged. **A is the designated primary** (it was R2's validation choice); B and R1-recal are reported in full. R1-shipped is reported on benign only, as a reference.
Thresholds are re-set by the R2 rule (smallest on the 0.005 grid with at most 3.0 false onsets per benign device-day) on **R5 validation benign** (section 4), per arm. The R2 thresholds (A 0.84, B 0.75, R1-recal 0.98) are also applied unchanged to the R5 test as a *transfer check*, reported separately (R2 showed thresholds do not transfer reliably: R1-recal went 2.88 to 11.7).

## 3. Which attack episodes are eligible (decided from documentation and label files only, before any detector runs)
An **episode** is a run of malicious activity on one device with gaps up to 60 s, counted per (device, capture). Episodes of the same device less than 60 s apart merge.
- **E1, onset provenance.** *Tier A (exact):* the onset time comes from a source independent of the traffic and of any detector: a published attack schedule or log with at most 1-second resolution, a per-record label written by the attack tooling, or an injection log. *Tier B (bounded):* the onset is only known to lie in a window, or the label was derived by inspecting the traffic. *Tier C:* the attack is already running when the capture starts (first malicious record within 60 minutes of the start), or the onset is unknown.
- **E2, clean prefix.** The same device, in the same capture or session, has at least **6 hours** of contiguous traffic before the onset (no gap over 30 minutes; a gap restarts the count), with **no malicious label** in it. The first 2 hours are burn-in and are not scored; at least 4 scored hours remain. A baseline from a *different* capture or day of the same device ("stitched") is **not** eligible for the primary analysis: continuity of the learned baseline cannot be vouched for.
- **E3, size.** At least 20 malicious ticks (100 s) from the onset.
- **E4, observable.** The attacked device maps to one MAC/host the R2 adapter can read, with network telemetry throughout the prefix.
- **Only Tier A episodes meeting E2 to E4 enter the primary analysis.** Tier B episodes are reported for episode recall only, in a separate row, never pooled and never in a delay estimate. **Tier C is not evaluated at all**: detecting a device that was compromised before monitoring began, with no clean baseline, is a different question and R5 makes no claim about it. The counts in each tier are reported.

**How onsets and delays are treated (the point of E1 to E3).**
1. Delay is measured only from a Tier A onset, in 5-second ticks from the onset's tick. Nothing is measured from "the start of the capture", from the first malicious flow of a Tier B/C episode, or from a label that cannot be dated.
2. **No lead credit.** A threshold crossing before the onset's tick is a false alarm, not an early detection (the onset is exact, so the 60-second lead tolerance used with IoT-23 does not apply).
3. **Pre-empted episodes.** If the score is already at or above threshold on the onset's tick (a false alarm that happened to be open), that episode cannot say anything about detection. It is excluded from recall and delay, **counted and reported**, and the primary result is re-computed with such episodes counted as detected and as missed, as a sensitivity bound.
4. **Censoring, not conditioning.** An episode not detected by its end (or by the end of the capture, whichever is first) is *censored* at that time, not dropped. The primary timing results are the share detected within 60 s, 5 min, 30 min and by episode end, and the Kaplan–Meier median time-to-detection with censored episodes in the risk set ("not reached" is a legitimate answer). The median over detected episodes only is reported once, labelled *conditional on detection*, and is never used in a decision.
5. Episodes truncated by the end of the capture are censored at the capture end and flagged.

## 4. Data and the split (decided now; assignment computed from identifiers, not from content)
**Step 0, feasibility inventory (metadata only).** Using each candidate source's documentation and label/schedule files (no traffic content opened, no detector run), a committed script counts the episodes in each tier and how many meet E2 to E4. Candidate sources, **not yet verified to exist or to satisfy E1/E2**: the attack-trace collection published by the UNSW IoT testbed group, which includes the same lab and devices as the R2 benign days and a documented attack schedule; any other public collection whose attacks are launched on a schedule against devices that were running normally before. Whether any source qualifies is the first thing R5 finds out.
**Feasibility gate (fixed now, not relaxable afterwards):** at least **30 eligible Tier A episodes**, in at least **8 distinct (device, capture) sessions**, from at least **2 attack types**. If it is not met, **R5's detection part is not run**, the result is reported as "insufficient eligible data", and E1 to E4 are not loosened. A claim about a single attack type needs at least 10 eligible episodes of that type; types below that are listed, not claimed.
**Assignment (by capture identifier, fixed before any content is read).** For each eligible capture, `sha256(capture_id) mod 4`: 0 = development (to look at behaviour), 1 = validation, 2 and 3 = **test**. Episodes of one capture all go to one split.
**Benign exposure for the false-alarm claim.** UNSW IoT-testbed days not yet opened, in calendar order from the dataset's list, skipping 16-10-12, -13, -14: the first 3 are *validation benign* (together with 16-10-12), the next 9 are *test benign*. Packet-metadata CSVs are used (the R2 adapter was checked against the pcap on a day-12 prefix and matched on all 14 active devices). All UNSW traffic is again assumed benign, which is an assumption. The sample is sized from R2's own dispersion (resampling its 31 device-day rates): 60 device-days would certify the upper bound of 3.75 only about half the time at A's R2 rate (about three in four for B), 120 about 70% and 90% respectively. The requirement is therefore **at least 120 benign device-days after the 2-hour burn-in**.

## 5. Measurements and decision rules
- **False alarms:** onsets per benign device-day on the test benign days, device-day cluster bootstrap (10,000) as in R2; alert durations as in R2.
- **Detection (primary analysis set, Tier A, not pre-empted):** recall within 60 s, 5 min, 30 min and by episode end; Kaplan–Meier median time-to-detection; AUROC on ticks that carry traffic. Per attack type and pooled; intervals by bootstrap over (device, capture) sessions, because episodes within a session are not independent.
- **Claim F, false-alarm budget (A).** PASS if the upper 95% bound is at most 3.75; INCONCLUSIVE if the point estimate is at most 3.0 and the bound is above 3.75, or if fewer than 120 benign device-days exist; FAIL otherwise. **The sample is fixed: an INCONCLUSIVE result is not repaired by adding days after the test has been seen.** It is a plausible honest outcome and is stated here in advance.
- **Claim D1, useful at this budget (A).** Pooled recall by episode end is at least **0.50** with the lower 95% bound at least **0.30**. These bars are set before any R5 data exists, with the R2 validation result (about 0.26) in view so they are not fitted down to what was seen. A miss is reported as "detects too little at this budget", not as a threshold problem.
- **Claim D2, not worse than recalibration.** A's recall by episode end is no more than 0.10 below R1-recal's, and its Kaplan–Meier median no more than 60 s later (or both not reached), by paired session bootstrap. Requires at least 30 episodes (the gate).
- **Claim B, better than recalibration.** Only if F and D2 pass and A's false-alarm rate is below R1-recal's with a paired interval excluding zero; if R1-recal itself passes F, the finding is calibration, not model.
- The sentence "the system detects attacks on real devices at an acceptable false-alarm budget" is supported **only if F and D1 both PASS**. Anything else is reported as exactly the claim that failed. A detector that lowers false alarms by missing attacks fails D2.
- B is reported on the same rules for information; no winner is selected on test data.

## 6. Secondary analyses (labelled, never pooled with the primary)
- **S1, Tier B episodes:** recall only.
- **S2, transfer:** the R2 thresholds applied unchanged.
- **S3, synthetic overlay (known onset by construction).** Attack profiles (per-tick bytes, connections, new remote addresses) are taken from the *development* malicious ticks of IoT-23 captures only, and overlaid on the **test benign** device-days: one episode per device-day, onset drawn from 6 to 20 hours after the day's start and duration 20 minutes, seeds fixed (`5000 + index`). It shows how the detectors react to a known profile with an exact onset. It is a simulation: it does not show attack coverage in real deployments, and its overlay code is committed before it is run. It is reported even if the feasibility gate fails, under its own heading.

## 7. Order of work (nothing is chosen on test data)
1. Commit this protocol. 2. Commit the eligibility/inventory script and its output (counts only), then apply the gate. 3. Compute and commit the split assignment. 4. Build the R5 adapters and drivers; debug on development-assigned captures only. 5. Calibrate on validation, commit the thresholds and code. 6. Record SHA-256 hashes of the test files, then run the test once (guard `data/results/R5_TEST_USED`). 7. Report every arm, including those that lose; freeze with a manifest and a tag.

## 8. Not claimed
Detection of compromised-at-start devices; coverage of attack types not represented; that UNSW lab traffic and its attack tooling resemble a real fleet; that all benign UNSW traffic is benign; detection by location, device-health or proximity signals or the fusion; that overlay results predict real-attack performance; that thresholds transfer to other sites. R3/R4 change no number in this experiment.
